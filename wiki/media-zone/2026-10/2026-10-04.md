<span class="mz-card-badge">Decision models · saved twice</span>
<h4>Building an RL fine-tuning platform for Jev-like decision models (Gero-4B)</h4>
<p>A build log that turns Qwen3-4B into a decision model, a model that scores options you give it instead of writing text. The architecture removes the language-model head, adds one tiny linear scorer, encodes the context once, and runs each option as its own branch that cannot see the other options. That makes the scores order-invariant by construction and works for 2 or 256 options with the same weights. Training has three stages with one job each: format on content-free synthetic data, then a calibration stage driven by an RL reward. The reward is the instructive part. The "obvious" reward (pay the model for how well its confidence predicts its own correctness) is not a proper scoring rule, and gradient descent drove the right answer's probability from 0.418 to 0.001. The fix pays log-probability of sampled real outcomes plus an honest-confidence term on the top answer, both proper scoring rules, so the optimum is the truth.</p>
<p><strong>Why it is for you:</strong> this is the most concrete open description of RLCD-style calibration training in the window, and it is a direct blueprint for a System 1 decider. The list of mistakes (a KL anchor that moves the optimum, Adam cancelling a gradient multiplier, a metric that rewarded overconfidence) is worth more than the result.</p>

<h4>Finetuning with Sampling: SFT learns better than you think</h4>
<p>Harvard's Aayush Karan, Sitan Chen and Yilun Du argue that SFT (supervised fine-tuning) generalizes badly because its data is off-policy, not because of its objective. Their fix leaves the loss alone and reshapes the data. They define the ideal target as the distribution closest to the base model that still respects the privileged information in the expert trace, then sample from it with Metropolis-Hastings, an MCMC method that proposes rewrites and accepts them by likelihood. The expert trace is the starting point and each step makes it look more like something the base model would write. Plain SFT on those rewritten traces rivals GRPO and on-policy self-distillation on chemistry, math and medical QA, and forgets less. Some chemistry tasks the base model never solved in 64 tries reach pass rates of 67.2% and 53.1%.</p>
<p><strong>Why it is for you:</strong> the saved post calls it "a rare principled free lunch" for midtraining and asks the right open question: for agents, is turn-level resampling enough or do you need environment replay?</p>

<h4>On-Policy or Off-Policy Learning? A systematic study of distillation dynamics</h4>
<p>A Cambridge study (Piskorz, Berthon, van der Schaar) that varies three things independently in strong-to-weak distillation: who generates the rollouts, the direction of the token-level KL loss, and the learning rate. The finding cuts against the day's mood. Rollout policy "does not necessarily play a central role". KL direction shapes task performance and output coverage, and learning rate governs forgetting and update sparsity. Forward KL is robust to who wrote the rollouts. Reverse KL is sensitive and prefers student-generated ones. On-policy data still helps on harder variants of the Countdown arithmetic task, but the advantage does not reliably survive a later RLVR stage (reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards).</p>
<p><strong>Why it is for you:</strong> it is the control experiment the on-policy story needed. Only the abstract page attached to the post was read, so no numbers are quoted here.</p>

<h4>AC2: Actor-Critic with Action Chunking</h4>
<p>GRPO needs every rollout to finish, because its advantage is the final reward minus the group mean. AC2 branches several chunks of tokens from a shared prefix and scores each branch with a learned value function, so the advantage is the critic's value of each continuation minus the mean across continuations. The critic is trained so its value of the prefix matches the mean value of the branches. The figure in the post plots IMO-ProofBench score against decoding compute and shows AC2 above GRPO and "Prefix GRPO" for most of the run. Tengyu Ma summarizes the three ideas: critics supervise the policy, LLMs allow easy resets, and the Q function shares parameters with the policy. Only the post and its figure were read.</p>

<h4>Notes on Jev: is Jev secretly a value function?</h4>
<p>Souradip Chakraborty and Amrit Singh Bedi separate two numbers that get confused. Selection probability says how often a policy picks action A. Correctness estimate says how often A is right. Reward maximization pushes the first to 1 even when A is right 60% of the time, so RL alone does not give calibration. A proper scoring rule (Brier or log loss) does. In sequential tasks with a success signal only at the end, the calibrated number you want is the probability the task succeeds if you take this action and continue with a given policy. That is exactly a Q-function. The router between System 1 and System 2 should then switch on the value of the best action, not on entropy across actions: "two good options" and "no good option" are different uncertainties.</p>
<p><strong>Why it is for you:</strong> it is a small formal model of when a fast decider should hand over to a slow reasoner. It is a hypothesis about Jev, not a claim about how Jev was trained, and the authors say so.</p>

<h4>Towards Looped Models Done Right, Part II: Rethinking at Fixed Points</h4>
<p>A looped language model reuses one block of layers several times per token, so depth grows without new parameters. The cost is that every loop adds work in training, decoding, prefill and RL. This paper's observation is that once the recurrent state settles near a fixed point, the path there stops mattering. That buys four shortcuts: truncated backpropagation, decoding that reads only the last loop's KV cache, a distilled student that prefills an 8K prompt 1.79x faster, and RL updates from saved states that run 2x faster than replaying the trajectory. Two training changes make the fixed points behave: a learned prior over loop depth, and orthogonal input injection. Mastery lens: this is the first account of why terminal KV sharing works, with a contraction argument.</p>

### Your attention kernel may be the bug

- FlashAttention-3 in BF16 trained healthily for 25B tokens, then gradient norm grew a thousandfold. No NaN.
- Cause: rounding breaks a zero-sum property of the softmax gradient and leaks the mean key.
- GProj restores it with two rank-one corrections per row, for 4.7% more step time.
- A second team reports backward precision issues in cuDNN, FA4 and flex attention, with a replacement kernel.
