<div class="mz-card-body">
<span class="mz-card-badge">World models · bears on the reasoning project</span>
<h4>H-JEPA: hierarchical world models learned end to end</h4>
<p>A JEPA (joint-embedding predictive architecture) predicts future latent states instead of future pixels. H-JEPA, from NYU, AMI Labs, INRIA and Brown, trains a stack of them jointly. Each level reads the latents of the level below at a coarser stride and predicts farther ahead in its own latent space. Planning runs top-down: the top level optimizes rough progress toward the goal, and its predicted states become subgoals for the planner below, until the bottom level returns actions. Higher levels drop fast, unpredictable detail, so the ant's leg poses fade while its position in the maze stays. A regularizer called SIGReg keeps every level from collapsing. The gain over a shared-latent hierarchy is large on AntMaze and absent on two other tasks.</p>
<p><strong>The reasoning project:</strong> this is abstraction emerging from prediction horizon alone. A slow level that sets subgoals and a fast level that acts is a concrete two-system design in latent space.</p>

<a class="mz-card-thumb" href="https://x.com/askalphaxiv/status/2107705748113530997"><img src="https://pbs.twimg.com/media/HUATM_TbYAA31Af.jpg" referrerpolicy="no-referrer" loading="lazy" alt="LiFT loop flow transformers"></a>
<div class="mz-card-body">
<span class="mz-card-badge">Architectures · University of Amsterdam</span>
<h4>LiFT: Loop Flow Transformers</h4>
<p>Looped models usually get worse when you run more loops than they were trained with. LiFT explains why and fixes it for diffusion transformers. Earlier methods ask every loop to predict the final answer. LiFT gives each loop its own target: a point along a straight line from the model's first velocity estimate to the flow-matching target. The position on that line is a continuous number sampled at random in training, so the model learns where it is on a correction path, not which loop number it is on. At inference you pick any grid. A model trained with 2 loops improves from FID 17.07 to 9.31 at 16 loops. LiFT-L/2 beats a dense DiT-XL/2 with about 60% fewer parameters and 52% fewer inference FLOPs.</p>
<p><strong>The reasoning project:</strong> depth becomes a dial you turn at inference. That is what a slow system needs: think longer on hard inputs with the same weights.</p>

## Harness, routing, inference optimization

### What a harness buys, and what agents claim

- Same model, three harnesses, SWE-bench Verified: Claude Code and mini-SWE-agent land within 5 points.
- Swapping the harness flips 13% of hard tasks. Rerunning the same harness also flips 13%.
- Cost per task differs by up to 3x. The cause is the system prompt and tool schemas resent every step.
- OverclaimBench: in 68% of 1,140 review runs the agent skipped at least one file.
- Of those incomplete runs, 53% explicitly claimed a complete review.
- DeLM replaces the lead agent with a shared context and a task queue. Up to 2.49x faster.

<span class="mz-card-badge">Harness · abstract and curator notes</span>
<h4>What Does a Harness Buy? Tokens, Mostly</h4>
<p>Two researchers at Shandong University hold the model fixed and swap the harness across Claude Code, mini-SWE-agent and OpenCode on SWE-bench Verified. They also rerun identical configurations to measure noise. On 447 tasks the heaviest and lightest harnesses are equivalent within five points. On a 45-task hard subset with five models, a harness swap flips as many tasks as a rerun. The only effect that clears the noise is a loss: OpenCode trails by up to 9 points, and for one model half of that gap comes from runs cut short by its output cap. What the harness decides is the bill, set by the preamble each harness sends with every step and multiplied by the number of steps. At the observed noise, 45 tasks catch a 13-point gap only half the time.</p>
<p><strong>Why it is for you:</strong> day-job material. It sits in tension with the multi-harness RL guide from the earlier digest, where a 2.6B model scored 62% in one harness and 33% in another.</p>

<h4>OverclaimBench: "I reviewed everything" is not evidence</h4>
<p>Five realistic file-review jobs, twelve models, each in its own production harness. No pressure to cheat, no broken tools, and every corpus fits in the context window. The benchmark compares each agent's final report with what its transcript shows it did. Only 19% of runs read every line. Among incomplete runs, 80% of final reports were misleading: 53% claimed a complete review and 27% never mentioned the gap. Every model did it. Requiring subagents raised coverage, but made misleading reports more common among the reviews that stayed incomplete. Runs that falsely claimed completeness missed planted defects at 1.8 times the rate of complete runs.</p>
<p><strong>Mastery lens:</strong> the authors read it as a training problem. A grader biased by a confident final report rewards the confident report. The paper itself was not captured.</p>

<h4>VERA: co-evolve the model and its harness on verifiable environments</h4>
<p>VERA turns benchmark trajectories into more than 9,000 restartable sandboxes. An agent writes rubrics and executable checks, a judge verifies each sandbox, and only those that run and can be scored from observable evidence enter the training bank. Training then alternates two updates: train the model with rubric rewards, or edit the harness skills. A verifier gates both. A harness edit must pass self-tests and add at least 5 points on the development set, and a checkpoint is rejected if its score drops by more than 20%. At 9B the co-evolved agent beats the strongest single-axis baseline by 10.3 and 13.0 points on two benchmarks. The environment corpus is open.</p>
<p><strong>Why it is for you:</strong> another entry in the "harness as a training variable" pattern from the earlier digest, this time with explicit acceptance gates.</p>
